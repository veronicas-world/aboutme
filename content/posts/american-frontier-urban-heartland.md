*My first time in Chicago last winter. Was quite struck by the sheer scale of everything, the buildings are far more cramped on the East Coast*

On the evening of July 12, 1893, a young historian from Wisconsin named [Frederick Jackson Turner](https://www.historians.org/person/frederick-jackson-turner/) stood up in Chicago and told the [American Historical Association](https://www.historians.org/) that the American frontier had closed. A short walk away, the [World's Columbian Exposition](https://www.chicagohistory.org/chicago-worlds-fairs/) was showing millions of visitors an entire city lit by electric light.

And here we are again. Four years ago, [GPT 3.5](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt/) launched and put the frontier of artificial intelligence on a screen and in everyone’s pockets. Today that frontier is leaving the screen and entering the now trendy world of “Physical AI.” Robot foundation models, vision-language-action models (VLAs) and world models are teaching machines to see and act in physical space. Consequently, much capital, talent and overall excitement have followed. One need not look further than their timeline on [X](https://x.com/) to see that this is true.

Another trend that has emerged in the post-GPT world is the issue of deployment. We have intelligence, sure, but the much harder work now is deploying and commoditizing it in a way that is quantifiably and verifiably useful to everyone and the economy overall. There is also a geographic element to this – frontier tech, at the foundation level, is built in San Francisco. This has been and will continue to be the case – but the markets for these products and what is built on top of them is NOT in San Francisco. It exists far beyond the worlds of Claude Cafes and it (the rest of the country) is a harder thing to conquer than the mission district. But anyway – we are in the deployment phase of software and have been in it for a while. This essay is not about that kind of deployment. I am not in the business of writing about the now and find the subject of the “next” far more intriguing.

So here we are. In the midst of a revolution of the physical AI space (at the foundation layer) and aware how hard deployment is to do well in software. My interest, and the topic of this essay, lies in both of these. While the foundation models for Physical AI will be trained in San Francisco, and they should be – the next hardest part of physical AI is, like software, deployment. This is the slow, necessarily local, and unglamorous work of making a robot do a real job in a real factory, shift after shift. And, moreover, I fundamentally believe that for deployment to work well, it cannot happen in one coastal city, and instead must be grounded in the American heartland. And Chicago (recall, the city where Turner declared the last frontier has closed!) has the best claim to it. Semper prorsum!

## Deployment is the bottleneck

Physical AI does not have a money problem. Robotics startups raised nearly $14 billion in 2025, a record and 70% more than in 2024, and 2026 has already passed that total in large sums. The smart folks at [Bessemer](https://www.bvp.com/) are calling this robotics' "GPT-2.5 moment" – which implies two things, 1) the capabilities are real and 2) the gap between lab performance and field deployment remains wide. So, as it currently stands, Physical AI has a last-mile problem.

To understand this more tangibly, think about what deployment here might entail: A robot packs as a crate. Someone has to install it, wire it into machines, make it safe around people, tune it to one plant's parts and lighting, maintain it, finance it, and earn the trust of a plant manager who has seen automation projects fail before. Etc, etc. Very hard.

But if deployment is the bottleneck, which it likely is, it is also the opportunity, as whoever builds the capacity to deploy well (keyword well) will capture much of the value the models create. And given the “physical” nature of this, it is inherently a geographic question as much as a technical one.

## Deployment can't stay in San Francisco

In software, a model can be updated from a lot of places, but in hardware a robot that fails or stops a production line needs a technician who can be on the floor soon. It needs spare parts nearby and an integrator who knows the plant and a lender who understands how a factory pays for equipment.

The companies closest to the problem have already organized themselves around this fact. [Formic](https://formic.co/) rents robots to factories and bundles the equipment, deployment software, maintenance, financing, field operations and performance monitoring into one monthly payment. It is headquartered in Chicagoland, close to many of its first customers in the manufacturing heartland. In October, Formic raised $50 million and announced it would work with [Physical Intelligence](https://www.pi.website/), one of San Francisco's leading robot-model labs, to adapt that company's foundation models for factory work using Formic's production data. *Many of you probably know Physical Intelligence, it is one of the hotter companies of our time!*

That partnership is a good representation of my central argument – the model layer comes from the Bay, but the data, the customers and the deployment must come from the Midwest. [Mind Robotics](https://www.mindrobotics.com/) is another good example. It is headquartered in Palo Alto, but trains its robots inside [Rivian](https://rivian.com/)'s plant in Normal, Illinois. [Mecka](https://www.mecka.ai/) is doing this from NYC and Toronto.

[![Private-sector manufacturing employment in 2022: seven states around Chicago had 3.42 million jobs, versus 2.26 million in California and Texas combined.](/writings/american-frontier-urban-heartland/manufacturing-jobs.webp)](/writings/american-frontier-urban-heartland/manufacturing-jobs.webp)

*The market for deployment is where factories are. Factories are concentrated in and around Chicago.*

The demand is also urgent. [Deloitte](https://www.deloitte.com/us/en.html) and [the Manufacturing Institute](https://themanufacturinginstitute.org/) project that U.S. manufacturers will need as many as 3.8 million workers between 2024 and 2033, and that 1.9 million of those jobs could go unfilled. A 2023 [NBER](https://www.nber.org/) study of U.S. factories found that robots concentrate in what the authors call Robot Hubs, and that one trait those hubs share is the presence of robot integrators, the firms that help manufacturers install robots. Deployment capacity does not follow a robot economy. It predicts one. The heartland's factories are short of people but not short of reasons to automate.

## Beyond "beat China"

A frequent argument you will hear from those on the coast in favor of the case for physical AI investment is we must “beat China,” The case for urgency is real – in 2025, China installed 354,000 industrial robots, 59% of all the robots installed on Earth that year and more than nine times as many as the United States.

[![Industrial robot installations in 2025: China 354,000, United States 38,500, Japan 36,219, South Korea 30,000, India 10,500.](/writings/american-frontier-urban-heartland/robot-installations.webp)](/writings/american-frontier-urban-heartland/robot-installations.webp)

Still, "beat China" is not enough to build on. The brilliant [Michelle Volz](https://volz.me/) was an early proponent of this, pointing out how fragile competition is as a motive; for example, when the Soviet Union fell, the urgency behind America's space program went with it. So while a rival can start an industrial policy, it cannot sustain it.

Volz's proposed alternative is to build "something worth fighting for, in places worth fighting for." I agree strongly with this notion and hope to extend it. The new factories she celebrates, in Tennessee, Louisiana, Utah and Alabama, matter enormously. But most of American manufacturing already exists and lives in nearly 400,000 establishments, almost all of them small or mid-sized. For most workers, the frontier will not arrive as a new gigafactory in their town, but will arrive through deployment into the plants they work in.

And, of course there is a careful conversation to be had around job loss – deployment does entail automation, which will eventually make some jobs obsolete. Rivian plant runs about 6,000 robots alongside 7,000 people, and advanced robots could eventually do all of those people's jobs, though of course, that will take decades upon decades. And in the case of Mind's robots – they will start with about a fifth of plant tasks, but eventually work their way to 80%. How do we treat this?

First, one must point out that the heartland's factories are already dangerously short of people, as the labor projections above make clear. Second, deployment is itself a job engine that fits the people already working there. See below:

- Mind Robotics' role in Normal is a Forward Deployed Engineer who works on site, starting with data-collection devices and moving into robot deployment support.
- Formic's business runs on field operations, maintenance and monitoring crews.
- At [Forterra](https://www.forterra.com/)'s autonomous-vehicle test range in Idaho, Volz found that only one of a dozen robot technicians came from a startup background. The rest were former electricians, lumber techs, medical-device repairmen and car mechanics.

My hope is that the people who have kept American factories running for generations become the people who teach machines. There is both a moral, patriotic imperative and an economic opportunity to anchor deployment in the heartland.

## Why Chicago

Chicago has every ingredient a deployment economy needs. The historian [William Cronon](https://www.williamcronon.net/biography.htm) called Chicago "Nature's Metropolis" because in the nineteenth century it became the hub that connected the frontier's raw materials to the markets that needed them: grain elevators, stockyards, lumberyards and, above all, railroads. Chicago did not invent the reaper or the locomotive, but it was able to capitalize so successfully because it became the place where inventions met the market at scale. That is what deployment is!

The same structure is still there:

| What deployment needs | What Chicago already has |
| --- | --- |
| Customers | About 400,000 manufacturing jobs in the metro area, the second-largest manufacturing economy of any U.S. region, and more metal and plastics production workers than any other metro |
| Logistics | A quarter of the nation's freight rail traffic moves to, from or through the region each day, on six of the seven Class I railroads; a quarter of Americans live within 500 miles |
| Engineering talent | [UIUC's Grainger College of Engineering](https://grainger.illinois.edu/), with its [Center for Autonomy](https://autonomy.illinois.edu/) and robotics research at the [Illinois Manufacturing Institute](https://manufacturing.grainger.illinois.edu/); [Northwestern](https://www.northwestern.edu/); [the University of Chicago](https://www.uchicago.edu/); [Argonne National Laboratory](https://www.anl.gov/) |
| Places to build and test | [mHUB](https://www.mhubchicago.com/)'s 80,000-square-foot prototyping lab and micro-factory, which supported more than 320 startups in 2025; [MxD](https://www.mxdusa.org/)'s working factory floor on Goose Island |
| Channels to small manufacturers | [IMEC](https://www.imec.org/), the state's manufacturing extension center, which works with more than 700 companies a year; the [Illinois Manufacturers' Association](https://ima-net.org/), with nearly 4,000 member companies and facilities; MxD's 300 business members |
| Capital and convening | [P33](https://p33chicago.com/)'s [TechChicago Week](https://gotechchicago.com/week/), with more than 15,000 attendees; [Automate](https://www.automateshow.com/), North America's largest robotics show; local funds from [Hyde Park Venture Partners](https://www.hydeparkvp.com/) to [Jump Capital](https://jumpcap.com/) |
| Proof the state can execute | The [Illinois Quantum and Microelectronics Park](https://iqmp.org/): a $500 million state commitment that landed [PsiQuantum](https://www.psiquantum.com/), [IBM](https://www.ibm.com/), [Diraq](https://www.diraq.com/) and [Infleqtion](https://infleqtion.com/) |

And the deployment wave has already reached Illinois. Recall – Formic is building its physical AI business from Chicago. Mind Robotics, backed by more than $1 billion, is training robots two hours south in Normal. In June, more than 50,000 people came to Chicago for Automate.

There is a gap, though. While Chicago has the customers, the freight, the engineers and the institutions, it does not yet have a cluster that ties them together. There is an opening now, but it will not be this way forever.

## What Chicago should do

Illinois has already shown it knows how to claim a frontier. [Governor Pritzker](https://gov.illinois.gov/about/the-governor.html) called quantum computing a field where Illinois has "a right to win," and the state backed that belief with $500 million, an anchor tenant and a startup on-ramp at mHUB. I believe Chicago should run the same playbook for deployment now.

Two recent experiments show what to avoid.

There has been much conversation around Austin’s tech boom and subsequent decline. In 2018, the [Army](https://www.army.mil/) put its new [Futures Command](https://www.army.mil/article/208477/army_announces_austin_as_the_home_of_new_army_futures_command) in downtown Austin, explicitly to be near the city's tech community. In October 2025, after seven years, the command was deactivated and folded into a new [Transformation and Training Command](https://www.army.mil/t2com). Some Army leaders still defend the original decision, but the lesson I take is narrow – one must put a buyer near startups and not create an isolated cluster.

Pittsburgh teaches the opposite lesson. It built a real, booming robotics cluster with more than 250 companies and 11,300 jobs, anchored by [Carnegie Mellon](https://www.cmu.edu/), and somewhat [Pitt](https://www.pitt.edu/). Yet when its startups raised about $1.5 billion last year, regional investors supplied only $23 million, down from $140 million a decade earlier. So it is clear that a city can grow the companies, but still export the upside.

Chicago's advantage is that it does not have to import customers the way Austin did, and it does not have to rely on only two universities the way Pittsburgh does. It has factories and needs a deliberate strategy to turn them into an ecosystem:

1. A landing program for deployment teams. [Intersect Illinois](https://www.illinoisedc.org/news/intersect-illinois-relaunches-as-illinois-economic-development-corporation-unveils-record-year-of-growth/) and [World Business Chicago](https://worldbusinesschicago.com/) should recruit the field operations, data collection and forward-deployed engineering teams of robotics companies, the Physical Intelligences and Meckas of the world, to base their Midwest deployments in Chicago. The quantum park's on-ramp can perhaps function as a template.
2. A host-factory network. IMEC, MxD and the Illinois Manufacturers' Association already reach thousands of plants. They could match robotics companies with manufacturers willing to pilot, with fair terms on who owns the data the pilots produce, so that a startup can get from a model to a factory floor in weeks instead of quarters.
3. A deployment workforce. Community colleges, unions, UIUC and Northwestern, UChicago, etc, can train robot technicians and forward-deployed engineers, so the labor gap becomes a career path instead of a crisis.
4. Local capital for deployment. Robots-as-a-service (RaaS) companies need equipment financing as much as venture capital. Chicago is a city of trading firms, lenders and funds. It should finance the robots that run in its own region, so the upside stays here.
5. The story. Automate already brings the robotics industry to [McCormick Place](https://www.mccormickplace.com/). P33 and TechChicago Week could make deployment Chicago's stated identity in the way quantum became Illinois's.

None of this requires Chicago to out-compete San Francisco at building models, but instead requires Chicago to be the place where those models go to work.

## The invitation and a humble disclosure

I do not build robots (I am also just a student) and will not be the one who decides where Chicago invests. That said, nothing excites me more than the frontier and because I have come to believe that its hardest problem is also its most democratic and patriotic one.

It has become clear enough over the past few years that models will be built by a few thousand people in a few square miles of San Francisco and occasionally New York. Deployment will take hundreds of thousands of people in thousands of places, and of every layer of physical AI, deployment is the one that can include the most of the country. The American frontier is at its core the conviction that there is always more to build and that ordinary people can be the ones to build it. My deep love of country stems from this and I want all of it to be part of the next frontier.
